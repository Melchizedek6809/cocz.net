ruxvt: what broke vibe coding for me

2026-10-25

A couple of months back, still having some tokens left in my subscription, I wanted to do a very small constrained project, a way to test whether AI (mainly Fable 5 and GPT 5.5 I think) are capable of producing excellent code.

For this I also wanted to have a project that has a lot of implementations available already, but none just the way I wanted them to be, the goal was mainly to have an efficient terminal emulator that uses as little memory as possible (I have 10 instances running most times), support native X11 and Wayland, idle at 0% CPU and support bitmapped BDF/PCF fonts.
Apart from that I wanted to use Rust here since the crates ecosystem is amazing, resulting in far less code for the LLM to write.

I had an initial prototype after a couple of session windows (no suprises here) and it actually worked quite well, leading me to use it as my daily driver.
After it completed a feature, I kept focusing on simplifying the codebase, basically by just asking AI to look at a specific file/module and come up with a plan on how to simplify it, approving it if it sounded sensible and commiting it if the diff looked reasonable.
I also ran it on various devices / tried all sorts of CLI/TUI programs to surface as many bugs as possible and just let AI fix things.

Now, keep in mind I've probably requested over 20 plans on how to simplify the various modules/files implementing most of them and at some point the answer was that the codebase is quite simple and there's very little to be done to simplify it.

I think it had ~7k LoC (I know, not a good measure but should give an estimate sort of). Some things were just odd that the LLM did not flag them, for example it wasn't using clap for parsing arguments. Got rid of hundreds of lines by replacing it with a super simple clap derive based parser (sure, the executable will probably get slightly bigger but that to me is kind of an obvious tradeoff).

Now for one of the more problematic ones, during development I had an issue where in sway/wayland it would only render the first frame and then not update, I described the bug and the LLM made a plan on how to fix this properly, this was probably a "mistake" on my part in how I prompted the LLM since it proceeeded to basically side-step winit by enumerating all screens, getting the refresh rates, getting the offset of the current window (of course only during startup, who moves a window). Calculates the percentage that each window occupies on a given screen and the proceeds to use the refresh rate of the display with the highest overlap as the refresh rate for the application itself, by just setting up a timer which then manually requests a redraw.

That whole machinery measured in at hundreds of lines, and on multiple occasions (think I asked both Fable 5 and GPT 5.5) when asked whether this was safe to remove it replied with huge text walls saying why this is important and absolutely needed to ensure proper functionality on Wayland and X11. So I just deleted that entire bit by hand and just hooked things up normally to just redraw when winit tells us to. Unlike the prior warnings on how this was required by state of the art models, removing it just improved things, idle CPU usage went down, it felt more responsive and I reduced the overall size of the codebase by at 15% or so.

This bit kind of made me loose all trust in LLMs for coding. I mean I could have probably told the agent to remove that bit, but I never would have noticed if I didn't look at the code manually. my guess is that a big problem was that it had surrounding comments warning about removal since this might silently break wayland, which the LLM apparently took rather serious.

Now at the time it actually did fix my wayland issues, took like 5 attempts to get things working there with increasing complex machinery. In the end after rewriting the entire redraw scheduling by hand (and removing lots of unnecessary indirection/abstractions) things just worked much better and without all that stuff.

This to me looked like an issue since quite often you have changes that by itself aren't bad, but they keep going in a direction that long-term lead to problems. Additionally it was concerning to me that the agent said that all of this was required, it was quite the mess to clean up since redraw was kind of spread out across 3 different files/modules but honestly wasn't that complicated. Something I'd expect a motivated junior dev to handle in a couple of days. Keep in mind the overall codebase was just a couple thousand lines of code with most of the heavy lifting being done in external crates.

What also suprised me was that I'd have expected LLMs to know better on how to use crates like winit, or how to handle GUI applications in general, I mean winit is a very popular crate with loads of open source applications and toolkits depending on it.

So this goes back to my now disillusioned look at LLM-based coding agents, most changes look alright or at least have a good enough explanation for why they are necessary. To ensure that you actually get a high-quality codebase you must manually go through every single line, think about each function and especially abstractions and indirections. The problem is that this takes time, a lot of time, enough that at least for me it doesn't seem to make producing great code faster. Additionally a big issue is that I actually enjoy writing code, so I get a modest speed boost but at the cost of the more enjoyable parts of software development.

I mean average software quality was already in a rather steep decline before modern LLMs got introduced, so might actually on average produce better code. Yet I have to say, in the last year I kept noticing more and more bugs and that decline in quality feels like it has sped up (although I have no data to back that up, just vibes).
